Circle Numbers marks a return to the framework that structures part of Arnecke's ongoing research. The project revisits the number 8 and its significance, now approached with greater clarity, precision and intent, consolidating ideas that previously remained in a formative stage.

In football, a number functions as a code through which the game can be read, associating role, movement and behaviour with each position on the pitch.

<img src="/images/research/pesquisa-1/p1.png" className="w-full" />

The study develops through three primary directions. The first examines the relationship between numbers and positional roles, exploring how these associations were constructed over time and how they continue to exist within the collective imagination. Running parallel to this is an investigation into football typography, considering its formal, structural and contextual variations within the game. Finally, the research extends to travel uniforms worn on matchdays, directing attention towards the intersections between fashion and football, particularly in the ways brands, clubs and players construct systems of presentation and association.

Throughout the research, a recurring observation emerges: certain positions appear to attract particular behavioural profiles. Not necessarily because of technical performance, but due to the intuitive relationship built around each role within the game.

In football, every position operates according to its own logic of presence. The goalkeeper exists in a state of isolation and vigilance, observing the match from a distance and reacting in decisive moments. Centre-backs operate through direct confrontation; midfielders organise the flow of play; while forwards concentrate expectation and projection. The pitch distributes functions, as well as distinct modes of action: protecting, articulating, controlling and deciding.

This reading appears across different interpretations of football throughout history. In Football in Sun and Shadow, Eduardo Galeano describes the goalkeeper as someone "condemned to watch the match from afar", alone between the posts, carrying a position marked by constant exposure and the unavoidable responsibility of error.

In another direction, the number 8 frequently appears associated with a broad reading of the game and a continuous circulation between different areas of the pitch. In Alexandre Alliatti's article for Globo Esporte's <a className='text-color-arnecke-blue hover:opacity-70' href="https://app.globoesporte.globo.com/copa-do-mundo/jogo-de-amarelinha/jogo-de-amarelinha-camisa-8.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Jogo de Amarelinha</a> project during the 2014 FIFA World Cup, the number is described as "an eyewitness to everything everyone does. Sees better than anyone. Sees more than anyone." The definition encapsulates a role that is not fixed to a single point, but instead accompanies the game through constant movement.

Over time, some numbers ceased to function merely as tactical references and began to carry identities of their own. Certain associations became so established that they transcended generations.

Johan Cruyff transformed the number 14 shirt into a direct extension of his way of playing. What began as a random number became synonymous with mobility, intelligence and freedom on the pitch.

<img src="/images/research/pesquisa-1/p3.png" className="mx-auto w-full" />

The same can be observed with other numbers throughout contemporary football. The number 19 is often associated with a player still in development, yet already projected as a future protagonist. Lionel Messi during the early stages of his career at FC Barcelona and, more recently, Lamine Yamal help reinforce this perception.

The number 21 has also developed a particular mythology within the game, frequently associated with midfielders known for refined technical control and spatial awareness. Players such as Zinedine Zidane, Andrea Pirlo and Paulo Dybala reinforce this association across different generations.

<img src="/images/research/pesquisa-1/p6.png" className="mx-auto w-full" />

The relationship between player, supporter and number extends beyond simple identification on the pitch. On the shirt, the number becomes a form of visual signature, instantly recognisable and capable of connecting player, club and public.

<img src="/images/research/pesquisa-1/p7.jpg" className="mx-auto w-full" />

To understand this process, it is important to consider the origins of squad numbering in football. Its introduction dates back to the early twentieth century, with examples such as the matches of 1928, when Chelsea FC and Arsenal FC used numbers on their shirts to facilitate player identification on the pitch. Initially tied rigidly to positions 1 through 11, numbers gradually detached themselves from these functions, opening space for more personal and interpretive meanings.

It is precisely within this shift that typography assumes a central role. Beyond their functional purpose, numbers carry visual expressions of their own. Typographic design records specific eras, contexts and graphic systems. In many cases, it is possible to recognise a season, a club or even a player solely through the design of the shirt numbers.

Designer Antony Barnett was responsible for developing several typefaces within football's graphic landscape, producing work for clubs including Real Madrid, Liverpool FC, Manchester United, Fenerbahçe SK and the Italian National Team. Despite being widely recognisable, these visual constructions rarely foreground the authorship of their designers, remaining primarily associated with clubs or sportswear manufacturers.

Alongside this, leagues such as the Premier League established their own typographic systems, creating visual structures that are both continuous and immediately recognisable.

<img src="/images/football/FUT 24.png" className="mx-auto !mt-0 !mb-0 w-full" />

In specific moments such as friendlies, collaborations and special releases, clubs began exploring this graphic potential even further. Paris Saint-Germain became one of the most recurring examples of this articulation between football, fashion and visual direction, employing bespoke typography and special pieces throughout different moments of the season.

<img src="/images/research/pesquisa-1/p11.png" className="mx-auto w-full" />

This convergence reveals a characteristic of football that often goes unnoticed. Like school, military or corporate uniforms, football kits exist to organise a system. They identify teams, distinguish functions and make the game legible to players, officials and spectators.

Yet unlike most garments designed to identify and organise bodies within a system, football kits never remained restricted to their original purpose. Over time, they became surfaces upon which rivalries, triumphs, defeats and forms of belonging could be inscribed, exceeding any purely operational logic.

It is precisely this contradiction that continues to attract the attention of fashion. Few objects reconcile control and projection, regulation and interpretation, with such precision. Conceived to organise the game, the football kit ultimately became one of the densest symbolic surfaces in contemporary culture.
